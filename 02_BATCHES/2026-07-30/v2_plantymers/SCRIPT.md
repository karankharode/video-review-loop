## The one thing the viewer leaves with

That the wrapper on a banana could one day be made of corn protein and rot alongside the banana — and that the thing making it strong is the same pulling motion a spider uses to spin silk.

---

## Hook

**Spoken (15 words):** "Five days at sixty degrees, and one banana is still yellow. Look at the wrapper."
**On-screen (4 words):** `Day 5 · 60°C test`

**The caveat is spoken, not just shown.** The underlying test ran at 60°C — accelerated, not a fruit bowl. An earlier draft carried that only in the on-screen text and the caption, which means every muted, text-blind or fast-scrolling viewer received a claim we never actually made. The temperature is now in the voiceover *and* on screen. Neither may be dropped for pace.

### Alternates considered

1. "This bag will be gone in a month. It's made of corn." — Strong cold-fact energy, but it leads with the degradation claim, which is the *least* visual part. Also risks sounding like an ad.
2. "The reason this banana hasn't browned is a protein from maize." — Accurate, but it gives away the method in the hook. The assigned mechanism is *result first, method withheld*.

### Why the chosen one

It shows an outcome the viewer can judge with their own eyes in under two seconds — two bananas, one browned, one not — and the last clause ("look at the wrapper") redirects attention to the thing the video is actually about without explaining anything. The condition is stated in the first six words, so the claim is complete before the image can be misread: this was an accelerated 60°C test, not a fruit bowl. A stranger scrolling without sound gets the same caveat from the on-screen line, and a stranger listening without looking gets it from the voice.

## Caption

A banana that stayed yellow for five days at 60°C — wrapped in corn protein.

Researchers in the Netherlands, China and Hong Kong dissolved zein (a maize protein) in ethanol, watered it down until it separated into a thick protein-rich liquid, then scraped or spun it. That mechanical pull flattens the protein chains into interlocking sheets — the same structural trick behind spider silk. No chemical crosslinkers.

The result is stronger than polyethylene, and 60–80% of it degraded in simulated soil within 30 days.

Caveat that matters: they've made about one square metre of it. This is a lab result, not a product.

Nature Communications, 23 July 2026 · DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-72645-9
