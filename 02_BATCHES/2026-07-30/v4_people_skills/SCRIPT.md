# v4 — What Are You Practising For?

**Track:** SAI
**Pillar:** Interview + Placement Prep
**Audience:** Final-year college students and early-career professionals in India
**Emotional register:** Warn ("wait, is this happening?")
**Structure:** Countdown (3 → 2 → 1)
**Hook mechanism:** Uncomfortable question to camera
**Visual mode:** Split screen / side-by-side comparison
**Length:** ~45s
**Mode:** Faceless — VO + avatar/graphics, no presenter shoot

## The one thing the viewer leaves with

The entry-level door is narrowing, and that is a market condition rather than a verdict on them — but the part of the process they still control is the conversation, and that is worth rehearsing out loud before the job rather than on it.

---

## Hook — first 3 seconds

**Spoken (10 words):** "If entry-level jobs are shrinking... what are you practising for?"
**On-screen text (3 words):** `Fewer entry-level seats`

### Alternates considered

| # | Spoken hook | Why not chosen |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Why do employers cut junior jobs, then hire on how you talk?" (12 words) | States the tension correctly but front-loads the answer. Nothing left to open a loop with, and it reads as a thesis, not a question. |
| B | "What did twenty-two percent of HR chiefs just quietly decide?" (10 words) | Curiosity-strong, but it dramatises a survey response as a decision. On a warn-register video that is exactly the framing that gets screenshotted and picked apart. |

**Why the chosen one wins:** it is a real uncomfortable question rather than a threat — it names the shift without telling the viewer their prospects are over. It also opens a loop that the last line closes with the identical phrase, so the countdown lands as an answer rather than a list.

**Runtime check (145 wpm):** 103 spoken words = 42.6s, plus the 0.35s hold on the hook ellipsis and three ~0.2s micro-beats after the countdown numerals = **43.6s** inside a 45.0s cut. Rows 2 and 3 swapped 1.0s between them because the 22% line grew to carry its qualifier; rows 6–9 each gained 0.2s from the trimmed retention line. No row overruns its slot.

**What changed and why:**
- **"at least one leader… stopped hiring freshers"** — the old line read "stopped hiring them", where "them" had no antecedent except "HR heads". Literally parsed, it said leaders had stopped hiring the HR heads. It now says who stopped hiring whom.
- **"a hundred and ten HR heads worldwide"** — the sample is global. The video is captioned for Indian final-years, and nothing in the audio previously said the survey wasn't Indian. One word fixes it.
- **The 25s line** used to run "Fewer first jobs... screened harder on the one thing nobody practises. Three fixes." Read straight through, that builds a false syllogism: fewer jobs → they judge how you talk → practise talking → get the job. The channel does not claim that speaking well gets you hired. The replacement names the market as a market, hands the viewer back their agency, and costs 1.3s less — which is what pays for the two accuracy fixes above.

## Caption

Two pieces of hiring research were published in the same week of July 2026. Gartner's survey — fielded in Q4 2025 — asked 110 heads of HR worldwide, and 22% said at least one leader in their organisation has stopped hiring for entry-level roles because of AI automation. Days earlier, Drexel's LeBow College published a survey of 600+ employers who ranked communication, willingness to learn and adaptability *above* technical and AI skill — because the technical part, they said, they can train.

Read together: there are fewer first jobs, and the ones left turn on a single conversation. Fewer openings is the market, not you. How you use the conversation you do get is the part that's yours — and that used to be something you picked up on the job. Both samples are global; the numbers are not India-specific.

Start with one spoken answer a day. Save this for placement season.

**Nothing in this script is marked [NEEDS SOURCE].** Both headline figures trace to named, dated, primary sources.

### Two things deliberately *not* said

- No claim that AI is destroying graduate jobs in India. Both studies are global/US-employer samples, and the voiceover now says so out loud ("a hundred and ten HR heads **worldwide**") rather than leaving an Indian-captioned audience to assume the sample was theirs. The script localises the advice, never the statistic.
- No implication that speaking practice is what converts a shrinking market into a job offer. The 0:25 line explicitly separates the two: "That's the market, not you." The three fixes are offered as the part the viewer controls, not as a causal path to being hired.
- No fluency promise, no day-count, no grammar claim. The fix offered is speaking reps, which is what SAI actually does.
